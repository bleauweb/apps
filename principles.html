How we build

Thirteen rules, worked out by getting most of them wrong first.

These apps are built for people whose attention, memory and energy do not behave the way software usually assumes — autistic people, people with ADHD, and anyone for whom an ordinary planner is one more thing to manage. That is not a feature list. It is a set of constraints, and the rules below are what is left after applying them for a couple of years.

Most of them came out of NeuRoutine, which is the oldest and the most opinionated, and they were checked against the other apps before being written down here. A few were learned the expensive way, and those say so. The last section is the honest one: where the apps do not yet do what this page says they do.

What the screen puts in front of you

1

One thing at a time

The front door shows the single next thing, not the list. A wall of items is not information, it is a decision, and the moment you need an app like this is exactly the moment you have no decisions left.

NeuRoutine opens on Now: one title, one Done button, and a quiet "then:" underneath. Meals names the next meal that is not logged and nothing else. Wiederfind shows one card. The list is always one tap away and never the first thing.

2

Quiet, still, and legible

Nothing moves that was not asked to move. No sound, nothing autoplays, no bouncing, no confetti. Colour never carries meaning on its own, and contrast is measured rather than assumed — every value sits in the code next to the ratio it was tested at.

All three apps honour Reduce Motion and largely have nothing to reduce. Two accent colours instead of one, because a single violet cannot both be readable as text and carry white on a filled button — the first one we shipped failed at 2.90:1 in dark mode.

3

Time has to be visible

An app for people who lose track of time must be able to answer what now, and how long have I got without being asked and without making a sound. A clock time is a number you still have to do arithmetic on; "in 25 min" is the answer.

The newest rule here, and the most embarrassing: NeuRoutine went two years showing only clock times, in an app built around time blindness. It now says "in 25 min", "on now · 20 min left", "10 min ago" — on the phone, the Home Screen and the Lock Screen. It rounds down, so it never claims more time than there is, and it goes quiet past eight hours rather than keeping score.

What the app asks of you

4

Capture must not be taxed

Every decision demanded at the moment of writing something down is a reason not to write it down at all. Ask for the thing. Ask for nothing else.

NeuRoutine's capture is two fields — what it is and what it will cost — where it used to be seven. A one-press capture lands in the inbox rather than on today, because when is a decision too. Meals will log a meal with no calorie count, because most of what anybody eats has never been weighed and demanding a number before food can be marked eaten is how a food app becomes something to avoid.

5

"Not right now" is a first-class answer

Most software offers done or nothing, which quietly turns every un-done thing into an accusation. A day can be let go of without that being a failure, and a thing can move without being deleted.

NeuRoutine gives it its own heading in every entry — skip just today, move to tomorrow, put it back in the inbox — all reversible, and a skipped day costs nothing and counts as nothing. A day that ends with the list let go of says "that is a decision, not a failure" rather than congratulating you. Meals has a safe-food roster one tap away, and clearing a planned meal is not drawn in a warning colour, because it destroys nothing.

6

Nothing is hidden behind a gesture

A gesture is only ever a shortcut for a control you can see. A swipe nobody can see is a swipe found by accident, and being surprised by your own planner is the thing these apps exist to avoid.

Written after an audit found the one place NeuRoutine broke it: deleting a step was a swipe and nothing else, on the app's only destructive control, in the screen meant for reading. It has a visible control now, and the swipe stayed as a shortcut for people who had found it.

What the app is allowed to say about you

7

Report, never score

No streaks, no grades, no targets, no rings to close. A number you can fall short of is a number that will eventually be used against you, and for anyone prone to all-or-nothing thinking a broken streak does not restart — it ends the relationship with the app.

NeuRoutine names the heaviest day in a fortnight ("Tue 4 Aug was 13") instead of scoring the fortnight, and reports counts rather than percentages, because "2 of 4" is a fact you can act on and "50%" is a mark. Meals records what was eaten with no target, no bar and no ring — the number is the number. Wiederfind has no streaks and no scores at all.

8

The app never decides to interrupt

The rule is not "never notify". It is that the app never makes that call. Where an interruption exists at all, the person chose it, once, knowingly, and can un-choose it.

NeuRoutine has no notification capability whatsoever and never asks for permission — for the person it is built for, even an opt-in channel is a decision to weigh on every visit. Wiederfind never notifies either. Meals has exactly one: a heads-up a few minutes before a meal, off until you switch it on, naming the meal and nothing else. All three are the same rule, and this is the distinction that lets them differ.

9

When it cannot see, it says so

An app that guesses when it does not know will eventually be confidently wrong, and being caught out once costs more trust than the guess ever saved. Do less rather than be wrong.

NeuRoutine's energy ring shows a dash rather than zero when it cannot reach the shared store, because zero reads as an easy day. A widget that cannot speak for the whole day says "Open NeuRoutine" rather than "all done". Meals' Health integration carries an availability flag and says so in a sentence when the entitlement is refused, and logging still works.

What you can rely on

10

Say what will happen before it happens

A rule should be knowable in advance, not discovered by being stopped by it. An unexplained refusal reads as a broken app, and it is indistinguishable from one.

Wiederfind's answer buttons show the interval each one will produce, and a locked step says why it is locked. NeuRoutine learned this later: a drag that would put a day out of time order still springs back without a dialog, but the rule is now stated under the day it applies to, before anybody meets it.

11

Predictable beats convenient

Things stay where they were. A screen opens the same way every time, controls do not appear and disappear, and nothing reorders itself out from under a finger. Cleverness that moves the furniture is not a feature.

NeuRoutine's planner always opens on the day, never on the view you left it in. Weeks start on Monday everywhere, whatever the locale says, and the month grid is always whole weeks so it never changes shape. Meals keeps the day in time order always, and a logged row does not jump. Wiederfind keeps one vocabulary throughout — a card is a card on every screen.

12

What you make is yours

No account, no server of ours, no analytics, nothing collected. What you make lives in your own Apple services and syncs through your own private iCloud, which we cannot read — and it stays behind when the app is deleted.

NeuRoutine's entries are ordinary events and reminders in your own Calendar and Reminders, editable in Apple's apps and useful if this one disappears tomorrow. That is the strongest form of the rule: not "we will let you export", but "it was never ours to hold". Where an app cannot use Apple's own stores, plain export is the obligation instead.

13

One app, one job

An app that does two things does the second one badly and makes the first one harder to find. A planner is not a journal. A meal planner is not a diet tracker. A catalog of what you own is not a wishlist, a price tracker or a social network.

This is the bar every idea has to clear before it becomes a repo, and it is the reason there are several small apps here rather than one large one.

Where the apps do not yet agree

These rules were written down after the apps were built, not before, so they do not describe all three equally yet. Naming the gaps is cheaper than pretending they are not there.

Two apps still cannot be searched

Rule 3 is about time; the same argument applies to finding things, and re-finding something you know you wrote down is exactly the memory work these apps exist to absorb. NeuRoutine gained search this week. Meals' recipe library and Wiederfind's decks still have none — in a card app and a recipe book, which are the two places it would help most.

Meals shows the time of the next meal, not how far off it is

The same gap NeuRoutine had until this week. "17:30" is arithmetic; "in 25 min" is the answer, and a meal planner for people who lose track of time is precisely where that matters.

One of the apps celebrates and the others do not

Wiederfind forbids exclamation marks in its interface on the grounds that they are loud. NeuRoutine finishes a day with "All done for today" and a party emoji. Both cannot be right, and the disagreement has never been settled.

Meals decides what your room is like

It opens dark by default rather than following the system, on the argument that a planner opening white at 17:30 in a dim kitchen is a flash to the face. That is a real argument, and it is also the one place an app here overrides a preference the person already set once. It offers Match system, so the override is undoable — but rule 11 says predictable beats convenient, and the system setting is the predictable one.

Wiederfind shares and the others refuse to

Not an inconsistency, but worth saying plainly: NeuRoutine and Meals rule out sharing entirely, because what they hold is your day and your eating. Wiederfind shares decks and lessons, because a course is content somebody wrote to be used by other people. The line is what is being shared, not whether sharing is allowed.

Why write these down at all

Because rules drift. Every one of the gaps above is a rule that was true of one app and quietly untrue of another, and none of them would have been noticed by looking at any single app on its own. NeuRoutine's own rules were audited against published research on executive dysfunction in August 2026; two of them turned out to be working against it, and both were fixed the same day.

If something here does not match what an app actually does, that is a bug in the app or a bug on this page, and either is worth hearing about: support@bleauweb.org.
